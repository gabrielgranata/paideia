# Paideia: A Working Brief on the Philosophical and Theoretical Foundations of a Reasoning-First Learning Platform

**TL;DR**
- The product's core claim — that reasoning is a capacity formed only through its own exercise — is not a metaphor but a precise philosophical commitment with a 2,400-year lineage running from Aristotle's *dunamis/energeia* and *hexis* through Dewey, Vygotsky, Schön, Polanyi, and now Vallor; this commits Paideia to a single architectural rule: the AI may surface observations, questions, and structural prompts, but every inferential move must be made by the student, because a conclusion produced for them is not, in any defensible sense, theirs.
- Recent empirical work (Kosmyna et al. 2025, Shen & Tamkin 2026, Gerlich 2025, METR 2025, DORA 2024) converges on the same finding from neural, behavioral, and organizational angles: passive AI delegation degrades skill formation, critical thinking, and even reliability of self-assessment — so "AI-tutor" patterns that supply conclusions are not just philosophically off-target, they are empirically counter-productive; Paideia's design is the operationalization of the one usage pattern that *did* preserve learning in the Anthropic study (active questioning and explanation-seeking).
- The theory does not resolve cleanly into a build spec; it leaves four real tensions — productive resistance vs. demo legibility, LLM interpretive latitude vs. pipeline discipline, ownership of the reasoning artifact, and teacher labor cost — that must be held consciously during design rather than papered over, and a small set of "fidelity tests" (does this feature force the next move on the student? does it leave a navigable trace? does the tool recede during use?) is the right discriminator between Paideia-aligned and tutor-aligned decisions.

## Part 1 — Anchoring Frame: Paideia and Phronesis

### Paideia, in Athens and after

In Werner Jaeger's three-volume reconstruction (*Paideia: The Ideals of Greek Culture*, 1939–1944) and Henri-Irénée Marrou's *A History of Education in Antiquity* (1948), *paideia* is not a curriculum or a method but a civilizational ideal: the formation of a complete human being through the sustained engagement of intellect, character, and civic capacity with the inherited culture of the polis. Jaeger's central move is to refuse the modern partition between "education" as institutional practice and "culture" as accumulated content; for him paideia is the process by which a person becomes a person of a certain kind, with the polis as both medium and end. Marrou supplies the institutional history that lets the ideal stand without floating: he traces paideia from Homeric warrior formation through the sophistic and Platonic-Isocratic settlement, and is explicit that classical education aimed not at the transmission of fixed knowledge but at the cultivation of critical faculties — what he calls, of Plato in particular, a "non-banking" model of education.

Paideia is therefore differentiated from two adjacent Greek concepts that the product must keep distinct:

- *Technē* — craft, skill, the codifiable know-how of producing a result. Technē is teachable as procedure and assessable by output. It corresponds to what an LLM tutor that "shows you how" is offering.
- *Epistēmē* — demonstrative knowledge, scientific cognition of necessary truths. Epistēmē is propositional; it can be written down and copied. It corresponds to what a search engine or a textbook delivers.

Paideia sits orthogonal to both. A student can pass a technē test (run the procedure) and an epistēmē test (state the proposition) without having undergone paideia, which is the formation of the *person doing the running and the stating*. **Implication for Paideia:** the artifact the platform optimizes for cannot be the essay or the answer; it must be the trajectory of the student's reasoning, because that trajectory is the only place paideia is visible. The teacher view, the assessment surface, and the student-facing affordances should all foreground that trajectory rather than the polished output.

### Phronesis (Nicomachean Ethics VI)

Aristotle distinguishes five intellectual virtues; the one that does the work for Paideia is *phronēsis*, practical wisdom — the capacity to deliberate well about what is to be done in particular, contingent situations where things could be otherwise. Phronēsis differs from *epistēmē* (which concerns the necessary and universal) and from *technē* (which concerns making, *poiēsis*, with an end outside the activity). Practical wisdom concerns *praxis*, action whose end is internal to itself, and it is exercised through *bouleusis* — deliberation about means to ends already fixed by virtuous character. Aristotle is explicit that phronēsis cannot be acquired by being told; it requires experience of particulars and the formation of the deliberating person. The *phronimos* — the practically wise person — is, for Aristotle, the standard of right action, not because they apply a rule but because their perception of the situation is itself trained.

The philosophical claim that matters most: phronesis is irreducibly first-person. Telling a student the conclusion a *phronimos* would reach does not transmit phronesis; it transmits a proposition about phronesis. **Implication for Paideia:** the AI must not deliberate *for* the student. It can lay out considerations, name absent perspectives, surface a tension the student has glossed over — but the act of weighing, of judging, of arriving must remain in the student's hands. This is the doctrine that distinguishes Paideia from a tutor that "explains the answer."

### Hexis (Nicomachean Ethics II)

In Book II, Aristotle gives the formative mechanism: virtues — both moral and intellectual — are *hexeis*, stable dispositions, formed through repeated activity of the kind they are dispositions toward. The famous formulation: "we become builders by building and lyre-players by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" (NE 1103a32 ff., Crisp trans.). This is a strong claim about causal direction: dispositions follow activity rather than precede it. Aristotle pointedly inverts the case of the senses (where we have the capacity first and exercise it later) for virtues and crafts: here we *first* exercise, and the disposition forms behind the exercise. The corollary is that what people are *habituated* to do — including being shown the answer rather than reaching it — is what they become.

**Implication for Paideia:** the platform must be diagnosed by the activities it habituates. If the dominant activity it trains is "ask the AI, accept its synthesis, lightly edit," the hexis it forms is dependence and accept-and-paste. If the dominant activity is "encounter a source, formulate a claim, defend it against an objection, integrate a counter-example," the hexis it forms is reasoning. Every interaction loop should be evaluated for what disposition it is training.

### Dunamis / Energeia (Metaphysics Θ)

Aristotle's distinction between *dunamis* (potency, capacity) and *energeia* (actuality, being-at-work) is the metaphysical scaffolding under hexis. A capacity that is never exercised is *dunamis* in a degenerate sense; in the fuller sense Aristotle develops in *Metaphysics* IX, energeia is prior to dunamis in definition, in time (because actual beings produce potential ones), and in substance. The capacity is constituted by, and intelligible only through, its exercise. Reading is not the capacity to recognize letters; it is the recognizing of letters in act. Reasoning, on this view, is not a faculty stored somewhere that can be inspected when dormant; it is the activity of reasoning, *while* it is happening.

This yields the strongest single line for the brief: a conclusion that someone else reached is not your reasoning, because it never existed in your activity. It can become an input to your reasoning if you take it up and work on it, but the cognitive value lives in the working-on, not in the having. **Implication for Paideia:** AI-supplied conclusions are, with respect to the student's reasoning capacity, ontologically empty. They do not partially form the capacity; they bypass the only mode in which the capacity exists. This is the metaphysical justification for the rule "never write the student's reasoning."

### Eudaimonia as activity, not state (NE I)

Aristotle defines *eudaimonia* (flourishing) as activity of the soul in accordance with complete virtue across a complete life (NE 1098a16, 1102a5, 1101a14). Two features matter for Paideia. First, eudaimonia is *energeia*, not a possession or a settled state — it is sustained activity. Second, it requires a *complete* life: not a moment of brilliance but the durable exercise of formed virtues over time. Education aimed at eudaimonia is therefore aimed at producing a person who will continue, on their own, to exercise the relevant virtues, including the intellectual virtues of *phronesis* and *sophia*.

**Implication for Paideia:** the success criterion is not the quality of the work submitted in the platform; it is whether the student, off the platform, continues to reason in the way the platform habituated. Anything that produces a strong artifact while leaving the student less able to do the work elsewhere is a failure even if every grade looks good. This is the long-horizon framing that makes the cognitive-debt evidence (below) load-bearing rather than merely cautionary.

## Part 2 — The Lineage: Theorists in Conversation with Paideia

### John Dewey

Dewey's account in *How We Think* (1910) and elaborated in *Democracy and Education* (1916) and *Experience and Education* (1938) gives the operational psychology that fills out Aristotle's hexis. Genuine thinking begins, for Dewey, in a "felt difficulty" — an undefined uneasiness or a perplexity that demands resolution; it proceeds through location and definition of the difficulty, suggestion of possible solutions, reasoning about their implications, and testing. Without the felt difficulty there is no thinking, only the random flow of associations or the agreeable confirmation of pre-existing belief. Dewey is explicit that reflective thinking is "more or less troublesome" and "involves willingness to endure a condition of mental unrest"; he warns that the easy path is to accept the first plausible suggestion and so end the disturbance.

This is the keystone justification for *productive struggle* in Paideia. It also names the precise failure mode of an AI that resolves perplexity on the user's behalf: it terminates the disturbance before the activity that the disturbance was supposed to produce has occurred. **Design implication:** the AI's prompts must hold open, rather than close, the felt difficulty — naming what is unresolved, surfacing a tension, asking what would count as evidence — never collapsing the difficulty into a tidy answer. Dewey aligns squarely with paideia and phronesis; the small tension is that Dewey is more confident than Aristotle that the steps of inquiry can be made explicit, which provides Paideia's licence for a typed, structured substrate.

### Michael Polanyi

In *Personal Knowledge* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966), Polanyi argues that "we can know more than we can tell": much of competent practice rests on tacit knowledge that is not articulable as rule. He further argues that all knowing carries a "personal coefficient" — a passionate, committed engagement of the knower without which the knowledge is not properly held. Polanyi's paradox (named by David Autor) is that critical components of expertise resist codification and so resist transmission as content.

For Paideia, this carves the design space cleanly. The platform can capture and operate on *articulable* tacit knowledge — the moves a student makes in framing, weighing, conceding, qualifying — by giving those moves a typed structure they did not have in flat prose. This is exactly what argument mapping does (see Twardy below). What Paideia *cannot* capture is somatic tacit knowledge (the surgeon's hands, the musician's ear) and the deepest form of indwelling expertise that Polanyi describes. **Design implication:** be specific about which slice of tacit knowing the substrate makes legible — relational, dialectical, structural — and resist the temptation to claim more. Polanyi also gives an honest account of why exhaustive rubrication of student reasoning is dangerous: pretending all knowing is explicit erodes the personal coefficient that makes knowing knowing.

### Lev Vygotsky

Vygotsky's *zone of proximal development* (ZPD) names the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with the support of a more knowledgeable other (MKO); learning is socially mediated and proceeds through the gradual interiorization of capacities first exercised in dialogue. Scaffolding (Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976) is the operational metaphor: support is provided in proportion to what the learner cannot yet do alone, and is withdrawn as competence grows.

The clean alignment with Paideia: AI prompts and teacher review constitute scaffolding within the student's ZPD, and the typed substrate is precisely a record of where the support was needed. The harder question — and one the brief must hold open — is whether an AI can *legitimately* occupy the role of MKO. Vygotsky's MKO is a participant in a culture of meaning, oriented toward bringing the learner into that culture; an LLM is, on Vallor's reading (below), backward-facing pattern extrapolation. **Design implication:** the AI in Paideia should scaffold in a structurally narrow way (observation, question, structural prompt) and the *teacher* should remain the cultural MKO. The substrate's primary job is to make the teacher's MKO role tractable across many students at once.

### Jean Lave and Etienne Wenger

In *Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation* (1991), Lave and Wenger reject the picture of learning as the acquisition of knowledge by an individual mind and replace it with learning as movement from peripheral toward fuller participation in a community of practice. The newcomer is "legitimate" because their participation, however limited, is real participation in the practice; it is "peripheral" because they do not yet take on full responsibility. Identity, skill, and meaning are co-constructed in the community.

The implication for Paideia is non-obvious and important. A solitary student writing into a document with an AI is not, in Lave-Wenger terms, learning at all in the deep sense — they are not participating in any community of reasoners. The substrate becomes the bridge: forks of student reasoning, cohort-level visibility of how others approached the same prompt, teacher annotations that propagate across students, all turn the platform from a solo tutorial into a (legitimately peripheral) participation in a small community of practice — initially the class, then the discipline. **Design implication:** cohort-level features (not just one-to-one feedback) are not nice-to-have; they are how the theory predicts the platform graduates from training to formation. The tension here is real: situated learning is skeptical of decontextualized cognitive scaffolding, and Paideia must be honest that a software substrate is a thinner form of community than the apprenticeships Lave and Wenger studied.

### Donald Schön

Schön's *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983) gives Paideia two distinctions it needs. *Knowing-in-action* is the tacit competence visible in skilled performance — the practitioner knows more than they can say. *Reflection-in-action* is the practitioner's capacity to think about their performance while doing it, to reframe the problem mid-stream when a move fails or surprises; *reflection-on-action* is post-hoc analysis of the performance after the fact. Schön's target was the technical-rationality model of professional knowledge that reduces practice to applying theory.

Schön justifies two distinct modes the substrate must support. In the document, while the student reasons, the AI's surfacings should support reflection-*in*-action: a structural prompt that disturbs a move just made, a flagged tension that prompts reframing. In the teacher view, looking back across the artifact, the affordance is reflection-*on*-action: where did the student get stuck, where did they leap, what move recurs across documents? **Design implication:** the artifact must be navigable in both modes. A single timeline view supports neither well; what is needed is an in-flow surface (lightweight, present-tense) and a retrospective surface (queryable, structural). Schön sits comfortably with paideia, with phronesis, and with Polanyi.

### Hannah Arendt

In *The Life of the Mind* (posthumous, 1978), Arendt revives Plato's definition of thinking as the soundless dialogue of the soul with itself (the "two-in-one"). Her motivating concern was *thoughtlessness* — the failure of inner dialogue she saw in Eichmann's banality — and her claim is that thinking, distinct from knowing, willing, and judging, is the activity that makes a person responsible to themselves. Without inner dialogue, judgment cannot operate; thoughtlessness is therefore the precondition of moral collapse, not merely an intellectual deficit.

For Paideia, Arendt names the most serious version of the harm an AI tutor can do. An AI that completes the user's thought eliminates not merely a learning opportunity but the practice in which the two-in-one is constituted. This is more pointed than the cognitive-debt framing: it is not just that the student learns less, but that a faculty whose existence depends on its exercise atrophies. **Design implication:** Paideia is, on Arendt's terms, a structural defense of the inner dialogue. The AI's surfacings should provoke the dialogue (here is something you have not yet considered) rather than substitute for it (here is what you should conclude). The product writing — every microcopy choice — should preserve the second person and the open question.

### Martin Heidegger

In *Being and Time* (1927) §15–16, Heidegger distinguishes the *ready-to-hand* (*zuhanden*) from the *present-at-hand* (*vorhanden*). Equipment in skilled use is ready-to-hand: the carpenter does not perceive the hammer as an object with properties, but reaches through it to the work. Only when the tool breaks, fails, or is conspicuous does it become present-at-hand — an object of explicit attention, foregrounded in a way that interrupts the work.

The implication for AI tooling is immediate and consequential. Most current LLM interfaces force the tool present-at-hand: the user must compose a prompt, evaluate a response, decide whether to accept, paste, edit. Each of these is an interruption of the work itself. **Design implication:** Paideia's AI surface should remain ready-to-hand — minimal-friction, peripheral, declinable, never demanding that the student leave the document to converse with an assistant. The activity of reasoning is the foreground; the AI is equipment. When the AI's prompts become more salient than the student's argument, the design has failed Heidegger's test, and probably also Dewey's (the felt difficulty has been re-located from the problem to the tool).

### Shannon Vallor

In *The AI Mirror* (Oxford, 2024), Vallor argues that contemporary AI systems should be understood not as nascent minds but as recursive epistemic mechanisms: backward-facing extrapolations from accumulated data that, when integrated into human practice, recursively shape what humans subsequently think and do. Drawing on Ortega y Gasset, she names the human task that AI most threatens: *autofabrication* — the lifelong, future-oriented project of making and remaking oneself. Her concern is not that AI will surpass us but that we will defer our self-formation to systems whose architecture is structurally regressive, mistaking the mirror for the future.

This is the contemporary philosophical articulation of the cognitive-debt failure mode Paideia is designed against. Vallor's argument supplies the strongest justification for the platform's central asymmetry: the AI may reflect the student's reasoning back to them, but it must not project a finished version of that reasoning forward, because doing so substitutes the system's backward extrapolation for the student's autofabrication. **Design implication:** any AI surfacing in Paideia should be auditable as a *reflection of what the student has put on the page* (or a structural question about it), not as a *completion of where the student was going*. The latter is the moment Vallor's concern becomes acute.

### Bildung (Humboldt and the German tradition)

The Bildung tradition — Herder, Schiller, Goethe, Wilhelm von Humboldt, Hegel — secularizes a Pietist concept of formation in the image of God into the modern ideal of self-cultivation through engagement with the world. Humboldt's formulation is the most useful for Paideia: the individual must "absorb the great mass of material offered to him by the world... and reshape that material with all the energies of his own activity and appropriate it to himself." Bildung explicitly inherits the harmonious development of human capacities from Greek paideia, but adds two things paideia did not foreground: the irreducibility of the *individual* path of formation, and the indispensability of the world's resistance — engagement with otherness, with the alien text, with what does not yield easily — as the mechanism of formation.

**Design implication:** Bildung underwrites the importance of Paideia working with *real source materials* the student must struggle with, not pre-digested content. It also justifies treating each student's reasoning graph as singular: the trajectory is the formation, and uniformity across students would be evidence of failure, not success. Hegelian Bildung adds a dialectical note that paideia in Jaeger's reading already contains: formation proceeds through encounter with what one is not.

### Optional supporting figures

**Nonaka's SECI model** (Nonaka 1991, Nonaka & Takeuchi 1995) describes knowledge creation as a spiral of socialization (tacit-to-tacit), externalization (tacit-to-explicit), combination (explicit-to-explicit), and internalization (explicit-to-tacit). Paideia's substrate is, in SECI terms, an externalization engine: it captures the student's tacit reasoning moves in an explicit, queryable form. The honest caveat is that SECI assumes a community in which the spiral cycles; a single-student document gets externalization but stalls at combination and internalization unless cohort and teacher loops close them. This reinforces the Lave-Wenger point about cohort features.

**Engelbart and Licklider** ("Man-Computer Symbiosis," 1960; "Augmenting Human Intellect," 1962) define a tradition that the current generative-AI moment has largely abandoned: tools that *extend* human cognitive capacity rather than substitute for it. Licklider's image was thinking with a computer "as you think with a colleague whose competence supplements your own"; Engelbart's was a system designed so the human took on harder problems, not the same problems faster. Paideia should be read as a deliberate return to this tradition — its lineage is not the chatbot but the augmentation system. This is a useful framing in the design prompt: Paideia is a Bret-Victor-via-Engelbart artifact, not a ChatGPT skin.

**Lucy Suchman** (*Plans and Situated Actions*, 1987) argues that plans are not specifications of action but resources *for* situated action, drawn upon and revised in the face of contingency. The relevance to Paideia: a rigid pipeline that mechanically routes student moves through fixed steps will fail in the same way Suchman's robot fails when the room changes. The substrate's typed structure should be a resource the student and teacher draw upon, not a script the student must traverse.

## Part 3 — Contemporary Evidence

The empirical literature of the last eighteen months has, with unusual unanimity, confirmed the philosophical worry that AI assistance configured as completion degrades the activity it appears to support.

**Kosmyna et al., "Your Brain on ChatGPT" (MIT Media Lab, arXiv:2506.08872, 2025).** A within- and between-subjects EEG study of 54 Boston-area university participants writing SAT-style essays under three conditions (LLM, search engine, brain-only) across three sessions, with 18 returning for a fourth crossover session. EEG showed the strongest, most distributed brain connectivity in the brain-only group, moderate in the search-engine group, weakest in the LLM group. Notably, LLM-group participants largely could not quote sentences from essays they had just submitted — evidence that the cognitive task they had performed was prompting-and-accepting, not writing. The authors frame the result as accumulation of "cognitive debt." Caveats: small N, single domain, short timeframe, and EEG connectivity is a coarse measure of "engagement." But the directional finding aligns with everything else here. **Implication for Paideia:** a successful Paideia session should look, at the level of cognitive engagement, like the brain-only or search-engine conditions, not like the LLM condition. The substrate should be engineered so that even with AI present, the student is the one doing the integrative work that produces strong distributed connectivity.

**Becker, Rush, Barnes & Rein (METR), arXiv:2507.09089 (2025).** A randomized controlled trial of 16 experienced open-source developers completing 246 real tasks in their own mature repositories, with AI tools (Cursor Pro, Claude 3.5/3.7) randomly enabled or disabled per task. Before the study, developers forecast a 24% speedup from AI; after the study, they self-estimated a 20% speedup. Measured outcome: tasks took 19% *longer* with AI. The perception–reality gap is the headline result for Paideia: even sophisticated users systematically misjudge whether AI is helping them. **Implication:** student self-reports of "I learned this" or "this helped" cannot be trusted as a quality signal for a learning platform. The substrate must give the teacher independent evidence of the student's reasoning, not ask the student whether AI helped. This is one of the strongest justifications for the typed substrate over a chat log.

**Gerlich, "AI Tools in Society" (Societies 15(1):6, 2025).** A mixed-methods study (N=666, surveys plus thematic interviews) finding a significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool use and critical thinking scores, mediated by cognitive offloading. Effect was strongest in younger participants; higher educational attainment partially buffered. Caveats: correlational, self-report critical thinking instrument, no causal identification. But the mediating mechanism — cognitive offloading — is the precise mechanism Aristotle, Dewey, and Vallor each predict. **Implication:** the design must reduce, not facilitate, offloading of the cognitive work that constitutes the learning. AI should be configured to *return work to the student*, never to take it on.

**Shen & Tamkin (Anthropic), "How AI Impacts Skill Formation" (arXiv:2601.20245, January/February 2026).** The most direct evidence yet, and from Anthropic itself. RCT with 52 engineers (mostly junior) learning the unfamiliar Trio asynchronous Python library; randomized to AI assistance or no AI. AI users scored 17% lower on a comprehension and debugging quiz — equivalent to roughly two letter grades — with the gap most pronounced on debugging. Time savings were not statistically significant. Critically, the effect was mediated by *interaction pattern*: roughly 20% of AI users delegated wholesale (paste-and-go) and showed the worst learning; users who asked follow-up questions, requested explanations, or stayed cognitively engaged retained substantially more. The control group encountered more library-specific runtime errors and apparently learned the language *through* those errors. **Implication for Paideia:** this is the closest the literature has come to a direct A/B test of the design philosophy. Paideia's mode of AI use is precisely the engaged-questioning pattern that preserved learning; the failure mode it forbids is the delegation pattern that produced the 17% deficit. The brief should treat this study as an empirical validation of the architecture and cite it directly to skeptical reviewers.

**Twardy (2004) and van Gelder.** Twardy's "Argument Maps Improve Critical Thinking" (*Teaching Philosophy* 27, 95–116) reports that computer-based argument mapping in the Reason!Able program produced critical-thinking gains on the California Critical Thinking Skills Test "more than tripling" the absolute gains achieved by other methods; van Gelder's later meta-analytic work (2015) reports a weighted average effect size around 0.8 standard deviations in high-intensity argument-mapping studies. **Implication for Paideia:** the typed substrate is not merely a UX choice; structural representation of reasoning is the single intervention with the largest documented effect on critical-thinking gains in the educational literature. Paideia's queryable graph should be designed as an argument map in the Twardy/van Gelder sense, not as a free-form note tree. This is also where Paideia has empirical ground that AI-tutor competitors lack.

**DORA 2024 (Google Cloud, *Accelerate State of DevOps Report*).** Survey of ~3,000 developers. For every 25% increase in individual AI adoption, the model estimates a 2.1% increase in self-reported individual productivity and a 2.6% increase in job satisfaction; but also a 1.5% decrease in team delivery throughput and a 7.2% reduction in delivery stability. **Implication for Paideia:** the gap between individual perception and team-level outcomes mirrors the Becker perception-reality gap and warns against optimizing for the felt experience of the user. The product should optimize for downstream outcomes (does the student reason well in subsequent unaided work?) over felt productivity in-session.

The convergent reading: the philosophical case is no longer speculative. Across neural, behavioral, organizational, and educational evidence, AI configured as completer degrades the capacities Paideia exists to form, while the engaged-questioning pattern Paideia bakes in is the one pattern empirically associated with preserved or improved learning.

## Part 4 — Tensions and Open Questions

These are not bugs to fix; they are real tensions in the commitments that should be held visibly during design.

**Productive resistance vs. demo legibility.** Dewey's felt difficulty and Aristotle's habituated activity both require that the platform *not* immediately gratify the user. But hackathon judges, users in five-minute first impressions, and even teachers under time pressure read friction as defect. A prompt that holds open a tension is, in the demo moment, less impressive than an AI that produces a polished response. The product must decide where it sits on this axis for which audience, and at minimum the demo path should make legible *what* friction is doing — surfacing the move the student made, the question that opened, the structure that emerged — rather than asking the audience to take productive struggle on faith.

**LLM interpretive latitude vs. pipeline-not-agent discipline.** The philosophy commits Paideia to never having the LLM render conclusions or write the student's reasoning. But the LLM, by architecture, is generative, and any prompt that is loose enough to be useful in a wide range of student situations is loose enough to occasionally produce an inferential move on the student's behalf. The discipline of pipeline-not-agent — typed inputs, typed outputs, tightly scoped prompts that cannot return prose conclusions — is the engineering correlate of the philosophical commitment. The tension is that more pipeline discipline costs flexibility and naturalness; more interpretive latitude risks Vallor's autofabrication failure. The brief's recommendation is to err toward pipeline.

**Whose graph is the learning artifact?** The substrate is co-produced. The schema is the system's; the moves and content are the student's; the inflections, annotations, and questions that shape it come from teacher and AI. There is no single right answer to who owns it, but the question is unavoidable for assessment, for portability, for privacy, and for what the student is forming. If the graph is fully system-authored, the student is being fitted to the system's ontology (a kind of technē mistaken for paideia); if fully student-authored, the substrate cannot do the structural work that gives it pedagogical force; if jointly authored, the joint authorship needs to be visible in the artifact itself.

**Teacher labor.** The philosophy commits the platform to the teacher as the meaning-maker: only a human MKO embedded in a community of practice can play the Vygotskian role properly, and only a teacher can render judgments of phronesis. But every drill-down minute is a minute spent. A platform that requires per-student-per-document teacher engagement to function is not scalable, and an unscalable platform is not paideia for anyone outside elite seminars. The substrate must do real labor-aggregating work — surfacing patterns across cohorts, flagging where teacher attention has highest marginal value — while resisting the temptation to automate the meaning-making itself.

**The bounds of what AI can scaffold without substituting.** Polanyi-style relational and articulable tacit knowledge can be partially externalized in the substrate; somatic tacit knowledge cannot, and collective tacit knowledge (the knowledge that lives in a community of practice) only partially. Paideia must be honest about which subjects and which kinds of learning fall within its competence. Argument-heavy disciplines (philosophy, history, literary analysis, much of the social sciences, parts of mathematics) are core; clinical judgment, craft, and embodied performance are not, or are at most peripheral.

## Part 5 — Implications for Prompts and Design

### Philosophical commitments for the design prompt (Claude Design)

The design language should encode the following commitments without translating them into vibe:

The activity of reasoning is the foreground; the tooling is equipment in Heidegger's sense, ready-to-hand and recessive. Visual hierarchy should make the student's writing the largest, brightest, most weighted element on screen; AI surfacings should be peripheral, dismissible, and never modal. Felt difficulty (Dewey) is preserved, not eliminated: empty states, blank fields, and unresolved tensions are first-class design elements, not friction to be smoothed. The substrate is an argument map (Twardy/van Gelder), not a notes app — types and relations carry semantic weight and are visible to the student as structure rather than as a form to fill in. The teacher view is a reflection-on-action surface (Schön): it should support pattern queries across documents and across students, not just per-document review. Cohort visibility (Lave-Wenger) is a feature, not an afterthought: a student should be able to see, in a structurally honest way, how others framed the same problem, because legitimate peripheral participation requires a community to be peripheral *to*. Microcopy preserves the second person and the open question; the AI's voice is observational and structural, never declarative or evaluative; it does not say "the answer is" or "you should conclude."

### Architectural and behavioral constraints for the code prompt (Claude Code)

The LLM in Paideia is a structured-output engine wrapped in a typed pipeline, not an agent. It must produce one of a fixed set of move types (observation, question, structural prompt, named-tension, missing-perspective, candidate-counterexample) and must not produce prose conclusions, summaries that close inquiry, or completions of student arguments. Inputs to the LLM are not raw chat turns but typed slices of the substrate (claim-with-context, tension-pair, source-passage-plus-student-claim) so the model's interpretive latitude is constrained at the boundary. Output schemas should make it structurally impossible to return a paragraph that begins "the answer is" — the API surface should not have that shape. The substrate is the system of record; the LLM is stateless across moves except through the substrate. Audit logs distinguish *what the AI surfaced* from *what the student wrote*, with provenance preserved at the move level so that no AI-generated text can be silently incorporated into the student's reasoning artifact. The system never resolves the student's stuck point on the student's behalf; if the model is uncertain about which structural prompt to issue, the right behavior is to surface the uncertainty as a question to the student, not to default to a confident completion. Latency budgets prefer ready-to-hand quietness over impressive verbosity: a 200ms peripheral prompt is paideia-aligned; a 4-second polished paragraph is not.

### Tests of fidelity

Any new feature, prompt, or interface decision should be able to answer these to count as Paideia-aligned rather than AI-tutor-aligned.

1. *The next-move test.* After this interaction, who made the next inferential move — the student or the system? If the system made it, the feature is tutor-aligned. If the system surfaced something that *forced* the student to make the next move, it is Paideia-aligned. (Aristotle on dunamis/energeia; Arendt on the two-in-one.)

2. *The trace test.* Does the interaction leave a typed, queryable trace in the substrate that a teacher could later drill into and that shows where the student's reasoning was, what was surfaced, and what the student did next? If the interaction is ephemeral chat, it is tutor-aligned. (Schön on reflection-on-action; Twardy/van Gelder on argument mapping.)

3. *The recede test.* During use, does the AI surface remain peripheral to the student's writing, or does the student have to leave the work to interact with the AI? If the AI is foregrounded, it is breaking the work in Heidegger's sense and is tutor-aligned. (Heidegger on ready-to-hand.)

4. *The off-platform test.* If a student worked in this way for a semester and then sat down without the platform to write a comparable analysis, would they reason better, the same, or worse than a peer who did not use Paideia? The Anthropic, METR, and Kosmyna findings predict that most current AI-tutor patterns produce worse off-platform performance; Paideia's value proposition is that its pattern produces better. Any feature that would predictably degrade off-platform performance fails the test, regardless of how good the in-session experience feels. (Aristotle on eudaimonia as sustained activity across a complete life; Vallor on autofabrication.)

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 (hackathon prototype, now):** Implement the typed-substrate argument map and the structured-output LLM pipeline first, before any chat surface or freeform AI affordance. The single feature that most differentiates Paideia from a Notion-plus-ChatGPT clone is the visible move-type schema. Demo it by showing a teacher view that drills from a prompt down to where each student's reasoning structurally diverged. Threshold to widen scope: until that view exists and reads correctly to a teacher, no other feature is on the critical path.

**Stage 2 (post-hackathon, weeks):** Add cohort visibility with structural-only sharing (students see others' move structures, not their prose). Add reflection-on-action surfaces for teachers that aggregate across the cohort. Run a small pilot with one teacher and one cohort (15–25 students) using a standard critical-thinking instrument (CCTST or comparable) at pre/post; treat <0.4 effect size as a signal to revisit the substrate schema, ≥0.6 as confirmation that the Twardy/van Gelder mechanism is operative.

**Stage 3 (quarter-scale validation):** Replicate the Shen & Tamkin design within Paideia: randomize a learning task (e.g., a humanities reading the students have not seen) to Paideia vs. Paideia-with-completion-AI vs. control. The platform's claim should be falsifiable in this design. If Paideia does not preserve learning at parity with controls *and* outperform completion-AI on a delayed comprehension quiz, the design philosophy needs revision; if it does, this is the empirical anchor for institutional adoption.

**Things that should change the recommendations:** if pilot data show that students self-route around the substrate to get conclusions from external chatbots, the architectural defense has failed and the response is integration-level (browser extension, document-import policies, teacher-visible provenance), not philosophical. If teachers cannot make the drill-down view legible without specialist training, the substrate schema is too ornate and should be simplified even at the cost of expressive power. If cohort features produce a chilling effect on student writing, the structural-only sharing constraint needs to be tightened further, possibly to teacher-mediated rather than peer-visible.

## Caveats

The empirical literature is recent and the strongest single-result studies (Kosmyna, Shen & Tamkin) have small samples (54 and 52 respectively) and short timeframes; their directional convergence is striking, but the effect sizes should be held provisionally rather than treated as fixed parameters. The METR and DORA findings concern professional developers, not students, and the generalization to learners is plausible but not direct. The Gerlich study is correlational and uses a self-report critical-thinking instrument. Twardy and van Gelder report large effects for argument mapping, but most of the underlying studies are quasi-experimental and were conducted before the LLM era; whether argument mapping retains its effect when an LLM is available in the same workflow is, to our knowledge, not yet tested. The philosophical lineage is largely Western and skews toward dialectical and argumentative disciplines; it does not straightforwardly carry to subjects whose core capacities are embodied or performative. Jaeger's reading of paideia has been challenged for Hellenocentric teleology; Heidegger, Arendt, and the Bildung tradition each carry historical and political controversies that, while not load-bearing for Paideia's design, should be acknowledged when their vocabulary is used. Where the philosophy is contested — most importantly, whether an AI can ever play the Vygotskian MKO role, and whether Polanyi's tacit knowledge is articulable in principle or only ever partially — the brief takes the more conservative side (the AI cannot fully play MKO; the tacit is only partially articulable), and the design follows from that conservatism. A more optimistic position is defensible but would commit the product to claims it cannot yet deliver.
